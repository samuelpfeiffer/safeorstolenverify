# Stolen-vehicle public records requests

Research date: October 2026. Everything below was gathered with web search only. Direct page fetches failed in this session, so a few portal addresses come from search snippets, agency letters reposted on MuckRock, or vendor pages, not from the agency's own page. Rows marked **verify** need a quick check on the agency site before sending. None of this is legal advice.

## 1. Plan

We'll ask about 23 agencies for their current stolen-vehicle data under each state's public records law. The list mixes eight state agencies that hold or report statewide stolen-vehicle data with fifteen big-city police departments in the highest-theft states and metro areas, as ranked by NICB's 2025 figures (released March 18, 2026). For each vehicle we want the plate, plate state, VIN, year, make, model, date reported, reporting agency and whether it has been recovered. We will never ask for owner names or other personal information. We'll send one short master letter that names the right state law, track each agency's deadline, follow up once if a deadline passes, and ask every agency for a monthly update or a link to anything they already publish.

The most important distinction: **a city department's own theft reports** (its records management system, or RMS) are ordinary public records under state law. **NCIC/state "hot file" data** (the FBI's NCIC Vehicle File, California's DOJ Stolen Vehicle System, Florida's FCIC, Texas TCIC) is shared under FBI CJIS rules, and agencies will often refuse to release it in bulk. So we ask cities for "records of vehicles reported stolen to your department," not for "NCIC entries." Florida is the one state that already puts its stolen-vehicle hot file online (single-item lookups only).

### Why these agencies (NICB 2025 data)

NICB's 2025 report counted 659,880 vehicle thefts nationally, down 23% from 2024.

Top 10 states by number of thefts in 2025 (NICB): California 136,988; Texas 75,269; Illinois 28,327; Florida 27,142; New York 24,206; Ohio 20,628; Pennsylvania 20,568; North Carolina 20,395; Washington 18,039; Missouri 17,496.

Top 10 metro areas (CBSAs) by number of thefts in 2025 (NICB, as reproduced by Triple-I): Los Angeles 53,911; New York 27,138; Chicago 24,299; Houston 23,659; San Francisco-Oakland 22,197; Dallas-Fort Worth 21,638; Philadelphia 19,117; Washington DC 15,204; Riverside-San Bernardino 14,111; Seattle 12,684.

By theft rate, NICB's 2025 data puts San Francisco-Oakland (about 477.5 per 100,000) and Bakersfield (about 477.3) at the top. Pueblo, CO appears first (576) in a FinanceBuzz summary of the same data, so the two sources disagree. Colorado, New Mexico and Nevada are among the highest-rate states in NICB's 2024 data.

Denver, Phoenix, Portland, Las Vegas, San Antonio, Albuquerque and Memphis were on the original target list. I did **not** find current NICB counts for those cities, so they are included because they are large departments in high-rate states, not because of a ranking I could cite. Phoenix and AZ DPS are also our home state. I skipped Ohio, North Carolina and Missouri agencies to keep the list to about 20; they are good candidates for a second round.

### Likely pushback

- **NCIC / state hot-file data.** The FBI CJIS Security Policy bars commercial dissemination of NCIC data and discourages bulk requests. State control agencies (CA DOJ, TX DPS, FDLE, CBI, WSP, ISP, AZ DPS) may say the data belongs to the system, not to them. Kentucky's AG has upheld denial of NCIC-derived records (13-ORD-127, about criminal history). Oregon's LEDS rules limit NCIC-sourced data to criminal justice use. The answer is to ask for the department's own theft reports, which are its records under state law. Florida is the exception: it already publishes its hot file for lookups.
- **Open investigations.** Every state has a law enforcement exemption: California § 7923.600, Texas § 552.108, Florida § 119.071(2)(c), Pennsylvania § 67.708(b)(16), and similar. A stolen car that hasn't been recovered is technically an open case. Counter-arguments: Texas must still release "basic information" about a crime (§ 552.108(c), *Houston Chronicle*). Florida never exempts basic facts like time, date and location. California requires disclosure of basic incident facts, though I couldn't confirm that the "property involved" wording is in the current § 7923.610/7923.615 text (**verify**). We are asking only for property identifiers, not narratives, witnesses or suspects.
- **Personal information and the DPPA.** The federal Driver's Privacy Protection Act (18 U.S.C. § 2721-2725) protects personal information from DMV records: name, address, phone, SSN, driver ID, photo, medical information. A VIN, plate number and make/model identify a vehicle, not a person, and are not in the statutory list. That is my reading of the text; I found no court ruling on VINs. Plates can be linked back to owners through DMV records, so some agencies may still balk at plates. If they do, take VINs alone.
- **Commercial-purpose rules.** Arizona (sworn statement, possibly higher "commercial value" fees), Illinois (21-working-day track, must disclose if asked), Washington (no lists of *individuals* for commercial use), and Colorado CCJRA (pecuniary gain form). Always say plainly that we are a company.
- **Residency.** Tennessee limits access to its citizens.
- **"We don't have to create a record."** Most states don't require agencies to build new lists. Asking for "an export of existing data" from their system usually works. California lets agencies charge programming or extraction costs for electronic data (**verify** the current section number).
- **Monthly updates.** No state requires an agency to fill a request going forward. Monthly delivery is a favor, so ask for it nicely. If they say no, put a monthly reminder on our calendar and file a new, identical request each month.

## 3. Request letter

### Master template

Fill in the four bracketed items from the variant list below. Word count is about 240.

> **Subject:** Public records request: current stolen vehicle list
>
> Hello,
>
> Under the [LAW NAME] ([CITATION]), I'm requesting [AGENCY]'s current list of vehicles reported stolen that have not been recovered, plus vehicles recovered in the last 90 days.
>
> I run SafeOrStolen, a small company in Peoria, Arizona. Our website lets people check whether a used car was reported stolen before they buy it.
>
> For each vehicle, I'd like the license plate number, plate state, VIN, year, make, model, the date it was reported stolen, the agency that took the report, and whether it has been recovered (with the date, if you have it). I do not want owner names, addresses, phone numbers or any other personal information, so please leave those out. If any field is exempt, please send the rest.
>
> An export from your records system as a CSV or spreadsheet would be ideal, sent by email.
>
> I'll pay reasonable fees up to $50 without needing to be asked. If it will cost more, please let me know first.
>
> Stolen cars are often recovered within days, so a single list goes out of date quickly. Could you send an updated list each month? If you already publish this somewhere, a link would work just as well.
>
> [STATE-SPECIFIC SENTENCE, IF ANY]
>
> Thank you. I'm glad to narrow this if that makes it easier.
>
> Samuel Pfeiffer
> SafeOrStolen
> Peoria, Arizona
> safeorstolenmail@gmail.com

For a state agency that runs NCIC or a hot file (CA DOJ, CHP, TX DPS, FDLE, CBI, WSP, ISP, AZ DPS), change the first sentence to: "...I'm requesting records of vehicles reported stolen in [STATE], as held in [AGENCY]'s own files. I'm not asking for NCIC data, and if part of what you hold can't be released for that reason, I'd still like whatever can be." For FDLE, add: "Your Public Access System already lets the public look these up one at a time; I'm asking for the same data in bulk."
