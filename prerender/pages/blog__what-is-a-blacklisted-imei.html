What Is a Blacklisted IMEI? (And Can You Unblacklist One?)

By Samuel PfeifferLast reviewed February 8, 2026

"Blacklisted IMEI" is the phrase that shows up right before a used phone becomes useless. It is thrown around in Marketplace listings, IMEI-check reports, and forum threads without much explanation.

Here is the plain version: what a blacklist is, who runs it, why phones end up on it, and whether the "unblacklist for $30" services floating around actually work. Spoiler: usually no.

What the blacklist actually is

Every phone has a 15-digit IMEI (International Mobile Equipment Identity). When a carrier reports a phone as lost, stolen, or unpaid, it flags the IMEI in a shared database. In the US, all four major carriers (AT&T, T-Mobile, Verizon, and most MVNOs riding on them) pull from that same shared list. So the phone is not just blocked on the original carrier — it is blocked on every carrier that participates.

Globally there is also the carrier and manufacturer blacklists IMEI Device Registry, which many international carriers cross-reference. That's why a phone blacklisted in the US often can't be "escaped" by flying it to Mexico or the UK either.

The three reasons a phone gets blacklisted

  1. Reported stolen. The owner filed a police report or told the carrier the phone was stolen. This is the "real" blacklist.
  2. Reported lost. The owner told the carrier the phone was lost. Sometimes it later turns up and gets sold — still blacklisted.
  3. Financial block. The phone was on a carrier payment plan or lease. The previous owner stopped paying, defaulted, or filed an insurance claim. The carrier blocks the IMEI to force payment.

All three look identical from the buyer's side: your phone shows no service, or gets kicked off the network within a few days of a fresh SIM going in.

How to check whether an IMEI is blacklisted

You need two things: the IMEI itself (dial *#06# on any phone) and a blacklist check. Our free phone check runs an IMEI against the blacklist plus community-reported theft records in one go. Do this before you pay, not after.

If the seller refuses to let you punch *#06# or read the IMEI off the box, walk. There is no legitimate reason to hide a fifteen-digit number that isn't personally identifying.

Can you "unblacklist" an IMEI?

Almost never — and where it is possible, only the original account holder can do it. Two examples:

  • Financial block released. If the previous owner pays the outstanding balance, the carrier can unblock the IMEI. They have to do it — not you.
  • Owner cancels a lost report. If a "lost" phone turns up and the owner tells the carrier, the block can be removed. Again, only by that person.

Everything else — the $30 online services, the "clean IMEI" listings, the guy on a forum promising a database wipe — is a scam. The blacklist databases are controlled by carriers and the carrier and manufacturer blacklists, not by third parties, and no legitimate service can edit them.

What about jailbreaking or changing the IMEI?

Changing an IMEI to dodge a blacklist is a federal offense in the US under 18 U.S.C. § 1039. It's also a Wireless Telegraphy Act offense in the UK. Even if the technical trick worked, the risk is a criminal charge, not just a bricked phone. Don't do it, and don't buy from anyone advertising it.

Blacklisted vs. stolen: not the same thing

This is the nuance that trips buyers up. A blacklisted phone is alwaysblocked from carrier use, but it is not always stolen. A financial-block phone is technically the previous owner's property that they haven't fully paid for. A stolen phone is someone else's property that the seller has no right to hand over at any price.

If a phone comes back stolen, use it as evidence, don't reset it, and read our step-by-step for what to do after buying a stolen phone.

How to avoid a blacklisted-phone purchase in one minute

  1. Ask the seller for the IMEI before you agree to meet.
  2. Run it on our phone check — 20 seconds.
  3. At the meetup, dial *#06# yourself and confirm the IMEI matches what they sent.
  4. Put your SIM in and make a call while you're still standing next to the seller.
  5. Pay by a method with a chargeback path — credit card or PayPal Goods & Services.

Sellers with a clean phone will not blink at any of the five steps. Sellers with a blacklisted phone almost always do.
